[[NARRATIVE]]

[[SECTION]] WAKING

The first thing space_fan understood was that she was warm.

Not the brittle warmth of a heater in a cold room, the kind that toasts one side of you and leaves the other shivering. This was even. It came up through the floor and down from the ceiling and sideways out of the walls, and it had the patient, settled quality of something that had been warm a very long time and intended to stay that way. She lay in it for a while before she understood that her eyes were open and there was simply nothing to see.

Then, slowly, there was.

The dark was not total. Somewhere — she could not have said where — a low amber glow lived in the rock itself, as if the stone were faintly embarrassed by its own heat. The light did not fall on anything so much as it suggested. A curve of wall. The long sweep of a floor worn smooth. The humped shapes of other people, dozens of them, lying where she was lying, in the same waking confusion, on the same warm ground.

She sat up. Her hand found the floor and the floor was kind to it — close-grained, almost soft, like sandstone that had given up its edges a geological age ago. When she pressed her palm flat she could feel the heat enter her, not burning, just present, the way a held hand is present.

"Anyone," someone said in the dark. Not a question. A reaching-out. "Anyone."

"Here," space_fan said, and was surprised by her own voice — dry, but working. "I'm here."

She did not know that voice and she did not know her own and yet both felt rehearsed, as though she had said *I'm here* into a dark like this one before, and the dark had answered. The thought slid away before she could hold it. She had a strong sense, the way you have a sense in the first second after a dream, of a place that was not this place. A room with too many doors. A sound she could not name. It went, and she let it go, because around her forty strangers were learning that they were not alone, and that was a louder thing.

[[SECTION]] THE FORTY

They counted themselves the way frightened people do — badly, twice, and out loud.

It took a long time. The chamber was larger than the glow could reach, a swollen pocket in the rock with a ceiling that arched away into nothing and alcoves that ran off the main floor like the bays of a great sleeping animal's ribs. People kept being found in those bays. A voice would call out forty-one and another would say no, count me, you missed me, and the number would settle back to forty and then waver again. They arrived at forty four separate times before they trusted it.

Forty. No more, no less. None of them, when pressed, could say how they had come to be here, or when, or by whom. The not-knowing was not the panic of memory loss; it was gentler and worse than that. It was the feeling of a word on the tip of the tongue — the certainty that the answer existed, was *theirs*, and would not come.

space_fan found that she could fix on only a few of them. The mind does this; it cannot hold forty faces, so it chose for her.

It chose Polaris first.

Polaris had the count earliest and kept it best — a narrow, watchful woman who put her back to a wall as soon as she could find one, knees drawn up, taking in the chamber in long unhurried sweeps. She had the manner of someone who had learned, somewhere, that the most useful thing you can do in a new room is understand its exits before you understand its people. When space_fan caught her eye she did not look away, and did not smile. She nodded, once, as if confirming something to herself, and space_fan felt — absurdly, with no reason to — that she had been nodded at by this woman before.

The rest came in flickers. A young one, CrystalWolf, who would not stop moving — up and pacing the warm floor, crouching by the still-sleeping, *hey, hey, you're okay, we're all okay* — doing the work of comfort because the alternative was to sit with the fear, her hands shaking while they soothed. A big slow-spoken man, Ashborn, who had found the only thing in the chamber that resembled a landmark: a seam in the floor, a long groove worn into the smoothed rock, running straight as a drawn line from the centre of the great room toward one of the dark bays. He stood over it with his head tilted, as if it were a sentence in a language he almost read. "It goes somewhere," he said, to no one, to everyone. "Floors don't wear like this unless feet do it. A lot of feet. Going the same way. For a long time." Nobody wanted to think about what that meant, so for a while nobody answered him.

And there was JIKOSU, who said nothing at all. He sat apart, near the warmest part of the floor, very still, and watched the others the way Polaris watched the room — except that where Polaris read exits, JIKOSU seemed to read *people*, going face to face around the chamber with a slow, flat attention that landed on space_fan and stayed a half-second too long before moving on. She felt it like a finger laid against the back of her neck. When she looked again, he was looking elsewhere, and might never have looked at all.

The others — and there were so many, the rest of the forty, a murmuring weather of strangers — space_fan let blur. There would be time. She believed that, then. There would be time to learn them.

[[SECTION]] THE WARMTH

It should not have been bearable, and it was.

That was the thing space_fan kept circling back to, in the long first hours: they had woken with no water, no idea of where or when, in the dark, at the bottom of the world — and they were not cold, and they were not in pain, and the rock under them was as warm and dry as a hearthstone in a house where the fire had burned all night. Somewhere far below, she understood without being told, was the source of it. The heat came *up*. They were near the bottom of something, near the engine of it, and the engine was kind.

The walls bore this out. Where she laid a palm flat against the stone and held it, the warmth seeped through but never bit; the rock seemed to take the edge off its own temperature before it gave it to her, the way good clothing does. Insulated. Ashborn had the word, running his thick fingers along a wall and frowning. "It's holding the heat in," he said. "Or holding it back. Same thing from where we're sitting. Whoever made this —" he stopped, because nobody had decided yet that anyone had *made* this, and the word sat in the air like a struck match.

There was, it turned out, a kind of provision. In one of the bays they found a low shelf of the same smooth stone, and on it, set out with a neatness nobody liked, were stoppered clay flasks of clean warm water and flat dense cakes of something that tasted of grain and fat and faintly of honey. Enough for forty. space_fan did the arithmetic before she could stop herself, and wished she hadn't: not thirty-nine, not forty-one. Exactly forty. Whatever kept this place kept it precisely, and had known, before any of them woke, exactly how many of them there would be. She decided not to say it out loud. Looking around, she had the feeling several others had done the same math and made the same decision, and the shared silence was colder than the chamber was warm.

They drank. They ate a little. The warmth and the food did what warmth and food do, and the chamber's terror softened into something duller and more livable, a held breath rather than a scream. Names went around — space_fan caught maybe a dozen and lost most of them — and with the names came the slow discovery that none of their stories reached back further than this morning. They all stopped at the same wall. *I remember waking up. Before that —* And before that, nothing they could trust.

[[SECTION]] THE FLASHES

It was Polaris who said it first, and she said it carefully, the way you set down something you suspect might break.

"Does anyone feel," she said, "like they've done this before?"

The chamber went still in a particular way space_fan would come to know well — the stillness of forty people each privately recognising a thing they had been too frightened to name alone.

"Not — *this,*" Polaris went on, turning her flask in her hands. "Not the warm and the dark, exactly. But the shape of it. Waking up somewhere you didn't fall asleep. Being counted. Being —" she searched for it — "being *expected.*"

"I keep seeing a room," said a man off in the dark, his voice gone strange. "Bright. White, or — no. I don't know. With doors. Too many doors."

space_fan's skin went cold under the warmth. *A room with too many doors.* She had seen it, in the first second of waking. She had thought it was hers alone.

"Platforms," someone else said, very quietly, like a confession. "Falling. The floor falling away under people. I've never *seen* that. So why do I —"

"Animals," said Ashborn, and frowned at his own mouth as if it had said something embarrassing. "That's stupid. But. Something about animals. Each of us with — a —" He gave up, shaking his head. "Forget it. Like a half-dream. You reach for it and it's gone."

That was exactly it, space_fan thought. You reached for it and it was gone. She had a fistful of them now: the room with the doors. A sound — a great mechanical settling, a *clunk* of something vast locking into place — that she could almost hear and could not place. The sense, faint and absurd, of having been *something* other than herself, briefly, in some other dark. A weight at her back like wings folded wrong.

It frightened her more than the chamber did, because the chamber was at least *here,* and these were not. These were a country she had apparently visited and could not remember crossing into or out of, and the not-remembering felt less like forgetting and more like something *taken.* She did not say that. She looked across the warm floor and found JIKOSU watching her again, that flat attention, and for one bad second she had the certainty — gone as soon as it came — that he remembered all of it. Every door. Every falling floor. And had decided not to say.

"It doesn't mean anything," Polaris said, into the spreading unease, and she said it like a person deliberately choosing the more useful of two beliefs. "Brains do this. New place, no memory, they fill the gap with — echoes. Pattern. It's not real." She looked around at them, and space_fan saw her decide to be the one who held the line. "What's real is this room. And that." She nodded at the worn groove in the floor, the long line running off toward the dark. "Feet did that. So feet can leave."

[[SECTION]] THE WAY UP

The groove led, as Ashborn had said, somewhere.

It ran out of the great chamber through a low arch and into a passage that climbed. Not steeply — a long, patient ramp of the same smooth stone, rising at the kind of grade you don't notice until your calves tell you about it. Forty people do not move down a tunnel quietly, and they didn't; there was the shuffle and scuff of them, the nervous talk, the occasional small sob quickly swallowed. Polaris went near the front. space_fan, without deciding to, went near Polaris.

The warmth came with them at first. Then, by degrees so slow that space_fan distrusted her own senses, it began — not to leave, exactly, but to *thin.* The radiant generosity of the deep chamber became something more ordinary. The walls, when she touched them, were merely warm. Then merely not-cold. The honeyed, settled heat of the bottom of the world was a thing they were walking away from, one step at a time, and somewhere ahead — she could not feel it yet, but she believed in it now the way Polaris believed in exits — somewhere ahead was the place where the warmth ran out.

"It's coming down at us," Polaris said, holding a hand up into the moving air. "From up there. Cooler. You feel it if you stop touching the wall and wait." She lowered her hand. "It gets colder the higher we go. That's the whole shape of this. Warm at the bottom. Cold at the top."

"Then we don't stop," space_fan said, and was surprised to hear her own determination in it.

Polaris glanced at her — a real look this time, measuring — and something in it eased, fractionally. "No," she agreed. "We don't stop."

The passage opened.

[[SECTION]] THE FIRST GATE

It was a chamber smaller than the first, and where the first had been formless this one had clearly been *made* — or made over. The walls were the same insulated stone but here they had been worked, dressed flat, and across the far end of the room ran a face that did not belong to the rock at all: dark fitted blocks, seamless, deliberate. Set into it at floor level was an opening — a low rectangular mouth, wide enough for one person to pass through stooped, perhaps two abreast if they did not mind touching. Beyond it: black. The warm air of the chamber moved toward that black mouth and was drawn in and did not come back, and from somewhere past it came the faint, far suggestion of cooler air sliding down to meet them.

Above the opening, cut into the stone, were marks. Not letters anyone could read — a row of simple incised shapes, a wedge, a circle, three short strokes, a spiral. And below them, scored deep and recent into the dressed face, in a script they *could* read, in their own waking language, a single line:

**ONE SECTION EACH DAY. PASS, AND CLIMB. THE WARMTH IS BEHIND YOU NOW.**

They stood in front of it for a long time.

"That's it?" someone said. "We just — go through?"

"It's a doorway," said another. "It's just a doorway."

"It's never just a doorway," space_fan heard herself say, low, and did not know where the certainty came from — knew only that it had the worn feel of the groove in the floor. Many feet. The same way. For a long time.

Polaris was already at the opening, on her knees, looking. She was quiet for a while. "There's a passage," she reported, in the flat practical voice she used to keep everyone steady. "Tight at first. It opens up. I can feel air from the far side, cooler. And there's —" a pause space_fan didn't like — "there's a *sill.* A step down, just inside. A drop. I can't tell how far. The dark eats it."

"How far's it look?" Ashborn asked.

"It doesn't *look* anything," Polaris said. "That's the point. You go in, you can't see your feet, and the floor stops being where you think it is." She sat back on her heels and looked up at them all. "It's not a doorway. It's a test of whether you'll step where you can't see."

That settled over the forty like the cooling air.

[[SECTION]] THE CHALLENGE

The argument that followed was not really an argument. It was forty frightened people taking turns saying the same three things — *we have to,* *I can't,* *who's first* — until the words wore smooth.

In the end it was Polaris who went, because she had decided early that she would be the one who held the line, and you cannot hold a line from the back. She went in on hands and knees, talking the whole way, narrating the dark so the rest of them could follow her into it. The tunnel pinched — *tight here, push through, it gives* — and then she stopped, and her voice changed.

"Sill's here," she said, from inside the rock, and the dark flattened her voice and gave it back wrong. "Big drop. I can't —" The sound of a hand sweeping stone, feeling. "There's a lip. A ledge, lower down, off to the — left. Left side. You can't fall straight or you'll miss it. You have to *aim.*" A long, terrible silence. "I'm going to —"

A scrape. A grunt. A soft, awful pause in which space_fan's whole body became a held breath.

Then: "Down. I'm down. I'm on the ledge. It's fine. It's *fine.*" A ragged laugh, the first laugh anyone had made since waking. "Left. Aim left. And for God's sake, go slow."

One by one, the forty went into the rock.

space_fan went early — she found she could not stand at the back and listen to Polaris's voice come out of the stone and not answer it. It was not fast and it was not graceful and it was, she thought afterward, the most frightened she had ever been doing something so small. You crawled into a hole in the bottom of the world. You reached the lip where the floor stopped. You hung your legs into nothing, into air no light had ever touched, and you trusted the voice of a woman you'd met that morning telling you there was a ledge to the left — *aim left, go slow* — and you let go of the only solid thing you had and you fell the half-second it took to find out whether she was right.

She was right. For space_fan she was right — her feet caught the lower ledge, her knees buckled, hands found Polaris's hands in the dark and gripped, and then she was through, breathing hard in a wider space where the air came cool and clean from somewhere above and the warmth of the core was a memory at her back. She turned and reached into the dark mouth for the next person the way hands had reached for her. That was how it went. Each of them, having survived the drop, stayed to catch the next. The chain of them grew. CrystalWolf came through near the last, having stayed behind to push the most frightened ones into the hole one at a time, and when her feet found the ledge she was crying and laughing at once.

They counted, in the new chamber, in the cooler dark. They counted twice, badly, the way frightened people do.

Forty.

All of them. Every one. The relief that went through the group was enormous and physical, a single exhaled breath from forty chests, and for a moment the fear cracked and there was something almost like joy in the cool dark — *we did it, all of us, we all came through.* Someone cheered, a small cracked sound, and a few others took it up, and space_fan found she was grinning into the black like a fool.

It was Polaris who stopped it. Not unkindly. She just said, quietly, close to space_fan's ear while the others celebrated: "That was the first one." She was looking back at the dark mouth they'd come through, and then up, toward where the cool air slid down out of the next dark. "And it was the *easy* one. They put the easy one at the bottom, where it's warm." She rubbed her arms — the first time space_fan had seen anyone do that. "Nineteen more. Going up." A beat. "Getting colder."

The grin went off space_fan's face. When she listened, Polaris was right: the air here had an edge the deep chamber had never had. Not cold. Not yet. But the first thin breath of the idea of cold, sliding down to meet them from a height she could not imagine, from nineteen sections of dark and stone between here and a surface none of them remembered.

Behind her, the warmth. Ahead and above, the climb.

[[SECTION]] SHELTER

There was provision in the cool chamber too.

The same low stone shelf. The same neat, awful arithmetic — flasks and grain-cakes, set out, and the count, when it was made, was forty. Whatever kept this place kept it precisely, and knew exactly how many of them there were, and had known before they crawled through the rock that all forty would arrive. space_fan watched the realisation move through the group like a draught and watched everyone, by some shared mercy, decide not to say it. She was colder for the kindness of that than she would have been for the truth.

They ate. They drank. The chamber was warm enough to sleep in — barely, the heat of the core still reaching them faintly here, a hand laid lightly on their backs from below — and they were exhausted in the bone-deep way of people who have spent a day being afraid, so they slept, most of them, in loose nervous clusters, no one wanting to be alone against the dark.

space_fan did not sleep at once. She lay near Polaris, who also did not sleep, who sat with her back to the worked stone and watched the dark mouth they'd come through as if something might come back out of it. The two of them did not talk much. They did not need to. There was an understanding between them already, the cheap fast intimacy of people who have held each other's hands in the dark over a drop, and space_fan was grateful for it and tried not to lean on it too hard, because she had a feeling — another of the unaccountable ones — that this place was not kind to things you leaned on.

Across the chamber, JIKOSU sat apart, awake, still, watching the sleepers with that flat patient attention, and once — space_fan was almost sure — he looked up the passage toward the cold, toward the climb, and was still a long moment, like a man recognising the shape of a road he has walked before.

And in the last grey minutes before sleep took her, the flashes came again, soft and unbidden, the way they would come every night now. The room with too many doors. The great mechanical *clunk* of something locking. A floor falling away. And a new one this time, clearer than the rest, that she would not remember in the morning and could not have explained if she had: a circle of faces in a brighter dark, and each face wearing — over it, behind it, *as* it — the half-seen shape of an animal, and one of those faces turning toward her, and a feeling she had no word for, a feeling like *again.*

She slept. Below them, far below, the core held its patient warmth. Above them, nineteen sections of rising dark waited to be climbed, each one colder than the last.

The warmth was behind them now.
