[[SYSTEM_STATE]]

[[NARRATIVE]]
They arrived without ceremony.
No countdown. No instructions. No voice from above explaining the terms of their situation with the calm authority of something that had done this before. One moment there was something else — sleep, absence, the unmarked space before a dream — and then there was this. Light that didn't come from a single source. Ground that held but communicated, through texture and sound and temperature, that it wasn't making any promises. Air that moved in ways that suggested weather without producing it.
Thirty people in three separate worlds, all waking to the same first question.
They would not have recognized it as the same question. They wouldn't have recognized each other at all — the teams were isolated not just spatially but atmospherically, each environment shaped from different material, different threat, different logic. One world built from stone and fractured sky. One from corridors and structured silence. One from momentum and collapse. Three distinct survival problems that shared, beneath the surface, the same architecture.
They didn't know that. They couldn't know it yet.
What they knew was simpler and more immediate: they had found themselves in a place that was not where they had been, with people they may or may not have chosen, and the place was not going to explain itself.
The animals had not yet appeared.
Everything that followed was, in one way or another, the beginning.
[[TEAM_A_ASTRAL_WARDENS]]
The sky above them had not decided what it wanted to be.
It wasn't storming. It wasn't clear. It existed in the uncomfortable space between — violet and amber wrestling at the horizon, clouds moving in directions that didn't agree with each other, light arriving from no fixed source and casting shadows that pointed the wrong way. The ground beneath their feet was fractured stone: wide dark plateaus separated by gaps of uncertain depth, the bridges between them looking geological, looking natural, looking like something that had not been designed — which was itself suspicious.
They had found each other quickly. In a broken sky world, ten people tend to consolidate.
Schwalbe was already moving along the plateau's edge before anyone had finished orienting, converting what might have been disorientation into systematic scan. He crouched at the gap between the first and second platform, studying the drop with the focused expression of someone translating fear directly into usable data. "The gaps aren't random," he said. Not a theory. An observation waiting on confirmation. "Look at the spacing. Widest on the north side, narrowest running east. Something is directing movement."
"Directing, or funneling?" Duccarian said. He was standing at the center of the group, turning slowly, watching all quadrants at once. He said it without challenge — the question was genuine, and it was a different question, and he knew it.
Nobody answered immediately. The distinction mattered, and they all felt it, and none of them could yet say why.
"Does the difference change what we do right now?" Ashborn asked. He was scanning the group, not the environment — taking inventory of people, not terrain. It was not the wrong instinct.
"Not right now," Schwalbe conceded. "But it might later."
"Then we hold it," Ashborn said. "We don't lose the question. We just don't let it stop us."
CrystalWolf had already moved to the eastern rock face while this was happening. She was working her hands along a thin thread of water rising against gravity — upward, defying sense, tracing cracks in the stone before spreading across a flat shelf above and pooling in a shallow natural basin. She watched it for a moment before touching it, learning its behavior first. Then she cupped her palms to redirect a trickle into a folded section of fabric she'd pulled from her own clothing. "Water collection is possible," she reported to the group without turning around. "Slow. Consistent. This is how we drink."
"How much, how fast?" Rally Mally asked. She had been helping the group count themselves, matching faces to names, making a quiet mental record. She had an organizational instinct that didn't announce itself.
"Slow," CrystalWolf said. "I'd estimate six hours to fill a single container. We'll need to establish rotation and containment before we use it."
"I can help set the rotation," Rally Mally said. "Tell me what you need."
CrystalWolf nodded, and that was the beginning of the first working unit within the team — compact, quiet, no ceremony.
Rally Mally paused for half a second longer than necessary after finishing the rotation marks.
The structure was clean. Efficient. Balanced.
Nobody had questioned it.
Nobody had needed to.
For a moment, something in her tightened — a reflex expecting acknowledgment that didn't come. Not because it was denied, but because it wasn't required.
She exhaled once, quietly.
Then moved on without saying anything.
"We should split collection duty," Ashborn said, loud enough for the whole group. "Nobody stands here for hours alone. Rotate. We keep people moving."
"Agreed," said Shroud. He had been quiet until that moment, and it was the specific quiet of someone who was processing rather than absent. He was crouched at the far end of the plateau, examining pale fungal clusters growing from the underside of a stone overhang — breaking a small piece loose, smelling it, setting it on his tongue without swallowing. A pause. He spat, cleanly, to one side. "This one is fine. Mild. Nutritionally present."
"What about the darker ones?" Toad asked. He had come to crouch beside Shroud and was looking at the cluster Shroud had pointed away from — dense, near-black, growing in a different pattern.
"Don't touch the darker ones. The smell is wrong."
"How wrong?" Rally Mally asked.
"Wrong enough that I'm not swallowing the pale one either, until I've waited ten minutes and confirmed I'm still thinking clearly."
Toad accepted this, and stayed crouched beside Shroud. "I'll wait with you."
"You don't have to."
"I know." He stayed anyway, watching the edges of the plateau in the meantime, cataloguing shapes. It was a gesture — present, useful, without requiring reciprocation.
It was Toad who found the injured, later, on the second plateau.
He had gone exploring the ledge below — lower than the first, accessible via a narrow stone bridge that looked like it had been placed rather than formed. He'd crossed it carefully, mapped the space beyond, and come back with Orphan Annihilator moving carefully beside him, one hand pressed against the stone wall for balance, one ankle held at an angle that said everything without requiring words.
"There's a gap between the first and second platform on the bridge approach," Toad said, to the group and specifically to no one. "Drop looks shorter than it is. OA came down wrong on the far side."
"It's not broken," Orphan Annihilator said, before anyone could ask. "I'd know if it was broken."
"You'd hope," said The Reddening Day — already moving, already crouching, hands coming out with the unhurried precision of someone who had done triage before and knew that efficiency was more useful than speed. "Take your weight off and let me see it properly."
"I can still walk."
"I didn't say you couldn't." The Reddening Day's tone was calm — not soft, but entirely without aggression, which was a specific kind of authority. "I said sit down and let me see it. Those are different statements."
Orphan Annihilator sat.
The group formed around this — not hovering, but present, redistributing attention between the medical situation and the terrain around them. Duccarian drifted to the edge of the plateau and continued his slow turning survey. Schwalbe moved to the bridge and examined its structure from above. space_fan stood apart from both clusters — not inactive, but thinking, watching the whole board.
"What do you need?" CrystalWolf called to The Reddening Day.
"Strips of fabric. Firm ones if anyone has them. And two flat stones — palm-sized, as smooth as you can find."
Ashborn was already moving toward the wall, hunting stones. Rally Mally had begun pulling at the hem of her outer layer, testing the fabric's tensile strength. Shroud produced a strip from an inner layer without comment and passed it over. The team was responding without being instructed to respond. That was its own kind of data.
The Reddening Day worked with the focused economy of someone accustomed to doing important things in difficult conditions. They checked the ankle's range of motion — slow, deliberate, watching OA's face rather than the joint, using reaction as the real diagnostic tool. "Not broken," they confirmed. "But it's a genuine sprain. Medium-grade. It'll hold weight if it has to but it's going to hurt, and pushing it risks making it structural."
"So I'm limited," Orphan Annihilator said. It wasn't a question, or even particularly a complaint. Just a fact being established.
"You're limited for now," The Reddening Day said. "This is treatable. I need to brace it and you need to stay off it for a few hours. After that we reassess."
space_fan had been listening to all of it. When The Reddening Day looked up and gave the small nod — manageable, not critical — space_fan turned from the sky and felt two competing approaches resolve into something better than either. The injury was real. The terrain needed to be mapped. They had nine able-bodied people and one who knew the second plateau better than any of them.
"Here's what we do," space_fan said. The group quieted — not because the voice was loud, but because it carried the particular weight of someone who had finished thinking. "We make camp before we explore further. But we make it together. Duccarian, Schwalbe — I need your read on the bridge. Can we get ten people across safely with one person who can't move well?"
Schwalbe had already been looking at it. "It's wide enough for two side-by-side. The weak point is in the middle third — the stone there is thinner. We cross in pairs, weight distributed. Should hold."
Duccarian nodded slowly. He was still watching the gaps between platforms. "It'll hold. I'm more interested in what's on the far side."
"OA can tell us," space_fan said, turning. "You know that second plateau. Tell us what you saw — where the overhangs are, what the shelter options look like, what we're working with."
Orphan Annihilator looked up, something shifting in their expression. "You want me directing while I can't walk properly."
"I want your eyes and your knowledge," space_fan said. It wasn't particularly gentle. It was direct and it was true. "We have ten pairs of working hands. We don't need yours right now. We need what's in your head."
A pause. Then something in Orphan Annihilator's posture changed — releasing rather than deflating, the difference between setting something down and being relieved of it. "There's a three-wall overhang on the east side of the second plateau. Deep enough for the group. The floor is level. Better surface than here, better protection on two sides."
"Any water?"
"I didn't check the eastern face. I was working on not falling when I got there."
"That's where I'd look," CrystalWolf said. "East-facing stone in this light — if the same water behavior applies, there'll be a seam."
"Can we get OA across?" Ashborn asked, looking at the bridge.
"Together, yes," said Toad. "Two people flanking, controlled pace."
"I'll take one side," Ashborn said.
"I'll take the other," Toad said.
space_fan looked around the group. "Anything else before we move?"
Schwalbe said, "We should establish a signal protocol before we split for any reason. Two sounds for attention, three for emergency. Simple."
"Simple," space_fan agreed. "That's the protocol."
They moved.
The Drake arrived as space_fan stepped onto the bridge.
It resolved from the atmosphere the way weather resolves into lightning — not from somewhere else, but from everywhere at once, sudden and already present. Storm-grey scales shot through with veins of electric blue that pulsed faintly, like something breathed through. Eyes the color of the moment before a strike — not malevolent, just enormous. It landed on the far end of the bridge with a weight that should have shaken the stone but didn't, and it regarded space_fan with the patience of something that measured time in units that didn't translate.
Behind space_fan, the team had stopped moving. Nobody ran. Nobody spoke. The bridge held perfectly still.
space_fan kept walking toward it.
You are making the right choice for the wrong reason, it communicated — not sound, not exactly, but comprehension arriving behind the eyes as though it had always been there. The distinction matters to the system even when the outcome appears identical.
space_fan stopped three paces from it. "Explain the distinction."
You included the injured member because their knowledge had survival value. You framed their contribution as a resource extraction. The system does not evaluate resource efficiency. It evaluates whether you include people when their value is not immediately apparent. You passed the test. You passed it for the wrong reason. That will matter later, when the value is not apparent even to you.
"Does the outcome change?"
The Drake tilted its head. The electric veins in its scales pulsed once — slow, deliberate, like emphasis. Today, no. The outcome is the same. Ask me again in three days. The system will have adjusted the test by then.
"What are you?" space_fan asked.
A presence that communicates what you need to hear rather than what you want to hear. The Drake's gaze moved briefly past space_fan to the team assembled on the bridge and the platform behind it. Your people are watching. They are deciding whether to trust you. Not based on this conversation — they can't hear it. Based on how you look when you come back.
space_fan turned around.
The bridge held them all.
They found the overhang exactly where Orphan Annihilator had described. The Reddening Day fashioned a brace from the strips of fabric and the smooth stones Ashborn had collected, securing the ankle with practiced care and communicating each step to OA as they worked — not condescending, informative. CrystalWolf identified a second water thread along the new eastern wall almost immediately, exactly as she'd predicted, and established a second collection point while Rally Mally set up rotation assignments for both sites, scratching names into the stone in a schedule that distributed the duty evenly.
Shroud completed his ten-minute monitoring period and reported that the pale clusters were safe. He began categorizing — edible here, set aside; unknown, flagged; the dark ones were not touched. He worked systematically, almost medically, without narration. By the time the first small meal was organized, there was enough to constitute something real, even if small. It tasted like stone and faint earth and something else that nobody could name but nobody objected to.
"Could be worse," said Ashborn. "At least it's not nothing."
As the work settled into rhythm, the conversations shifted. Not strategy. Something else.
"Toad," said Rally Mally, sounding genuinely curious in a way she hadn't had bandwidth for until now, "where did you get that name?"
Toad looked up from the stone he was shaping into a rough food surface. "It's a long story."
"We appear to have time."
"No, I mean it's actually a long story. And strange."
"Even better."
What followed was long enough and strange enough — involving, apparently, a bet, a creek, a formal certificate, and a misunderstanding with a neighbor's goat — that real laughter happened. Brief and genuine and surprising to everyone who produced it, as though they hadn't expected it to still work, and were quietly relieved to find that it did. "I didn't expect that to still work," CrystalWolf said quietly. She covered her mouth with one hand and still laughed. The Reddening Day made no sound but their shoulders shook. Even Shroud, who had the expression of someone for whom laughter required a certain calibration, made a sound in the back of his throat that was the precursor to it.
Orphan Annihilator, sitting with their ankle braced and elevated, laughed the loudest. Nobody commented on this. It felt right.
Duccarian had settled at the edge of the overhang, overlooking the gaps between plateaus. He'd been watching them since they arrived — not anxiously, but attentively, the way you watch something that hasn't moved yet but you suspect is going to.
"Something is funneling us," he said again, quiet enough that it was half to himself. He'd said it earlier and been left without a direct response. He was saying it again not as a demand for agreement but because it kept being true.
For a brief moment, the gaps stop being stone.
They become lines on paper — uneven, sketched too quickly, corrected too late.
Frodge had leaned over his shoulder once, tapping the page twice.
"You're not drawing paths," he had said. "You're drawing where something wants you to go."
Duccarian had dismissed it then.
He didn't dismiss it now.
This time, Schwalbe looked over from where he was reinforcing the camp's edge. "Noted," he said. "I'm not dismissing it."
"I'm not asking you to confirm it," Duccarian said. "I'm saying it so someone else hears it."
Ashborn, from across the shelter: "We all hear it."
Duccarian nodded once, and went back to watching.
The light shifted. The sky did not stabilize — but something in its movement changed, marginally, almost like attention. As though it had been observing something and had registered a result. Nobody noticed this except space_fan, who noticed it and chose not to name it, storing it instead in the same place they'd stored the Drake's last words.
Three days, it had said.
They had two days before whatever that meant.

The labyrinth had walls.
The walls were the least interesting thing about it.
They were logical — stone, approximately two and a half meters high, consistent and unambiguous, arranged in patterns that rewarded careful observation and punished impatience. The kind of design that was not trying to hide itself. CharliSpirit had identified the base grid structure within forty minutes of their arrival, scratching a rough map into the dirt floor at junctions while she moved. lumallie was three steps behind her at every point, confirming or correcting, adding secondary detail where CharliSpirit moved fast and instinctive. They worked with the complementary efficiency of a dual system — two distinct processing styles that agreed on the same output.
"Main corridor runs north-south," CharliSpirit said at a junction, marking a long line in the dirt. "Branches off every twelve paces. Regular interval. Consistent width. Whoever built this wasn't trying to deceive."
"Or they were," Galaxy said, from where she was studying the wall texture at the junction's left face. She ran two fingers along the stone, frowning slightly. "And the deception isn't in the structure."
"It's in the objective," Godly_Aura said, which stopped the group briefly. He was at the center of the formation, and when he spoke it was with the directness of someone who had decided that naming problems was better than circling them. "We don't actually know what we're doing here. We know where we're going — roughly — but we haven't defined what arrival means. What success looks like. Whether reaching the center is the goal or just the next question."
"Reaching it answers more than we know now," said Polaris. He was already facing the next corridor, weight slightly forward, the posture of someone who found stillness uncomfortable for reasons he'd probably never had to explain. "We move, we learn. We stay, we don't."
"Moving in the wrong direction produces negative information," Revenant said. He wasn't hostile about it — his objections were always structurally sound, which was what made them worth engaging. "We lose time, we lose resources, we potentially activate threats we haven't assessed. The correct position isn't motion for its own sake."
"The correct position isn't stasis for its own sake either," Polaris said.
"I didn't claim it was. I claimed that direction matters and we don't have enough information to commit to one."
Elv was standing off to one side of the argument with the particular expression of someone grading it as it happened — following each point, storing each response, not yet ready to render a verdict. lumallie continued adding detail to the map as the debate ran, keeping her function going regardless of the outcome. Loyal2Schwab had crouched at the junction and was scratching a small symbol into the base of the wall — a mark that identified the junction by number, his own system, developed without announcement and already three junctions deep.
"What are you marking?" Golden asked him, watching.
"Junctions," Loyal2Schwab said. "So we don't recross what we've already crossed. And so we know which way we came in, if we need to reverse."
"Good instinct," Golden said simply, and said nothing else. Golden used words the way a careful person used rare materials — sparingly, because the scarcity gave them weight.
Ninnginni had been listening to all of it without participating yet. She was positioned at the center of the group, which she seemed to occupy as a matter of function rather than ego — she wasn't performing leadership, she was performing the role of a hub that the spokes could connect to. She was building something in her head, and it wasn't finished yet.
"The argument is real," she said finally, which acknowledged both sides without belonging to either. "Polaris is right that motion generates data. Revenant is right that direction matters. We need both, which means we need a method."
"That's what I was saying," Revenant said.
"I know," said Ninnginni. "I'm about to give you one."
Soup arrived between one moment and the next.
It was a presence before it was a shape — a sense of something vast fitting itself into a modest volume, then the shape itself, resolving with the quiet confidence of something that had made peace with its form long ago: a softly luminous sphere, roughly melon-sized, rotating with absolute composure at eye level. It produced no sound. It simply was, with the completeness of something that had been waiting rather than arrived.
The group stilled around it. CharliSpirit's stylus paused above the map. Polaris, notably, did not step back.
You are debating the wrong question, Soup said, and the communication arrived simultaneously in every mind present — not broadcast, but deposited, like information that had always been there and had only now been indexed. You keep asking: what is the fastest route? The system is not measuring speed.
"Then what is it measuring?" lumallie asked. She said it practically — the way she asked everything, like she was requesting a data point, not a revelation.
Your decision hierarchy. Specifically: who decides when the Floofs cannot agree, and why, and by what protocol. You have a functional map, a genuine disagreement, and no framework for resolution. The labyrinth's center will force this question under significant pressure. I recommend you answer it now, when the pressure is only the pressure you're generating yourself.
"You're saying navigation was never the real challenge," Galaxy said slowly, turning from the wall.
I am saying that the navigation was the surface. A pause in the rotation. I will not tell you what the center contains. I will tell you that what you choose to do when you arrive will depend entirely on who among you speaks last in a difficult moment, and whether the others trust that this is the right person, and whether they have decided in advance to accept the outcome.
"How do we decide in advance to accept something we haven't seen?" Godly_Aura asked.
That is the question. Soup drifted slightly upward — not leaving, but adjusting, gaining some perspective on the assembled group the way someone steps back to see a full painting. The answer is that you don't accept an outcome. You accept a process. If the process is sound and agreed-upon, the outcome becomes something you can work with regardless of whether you'd have chosen it individually.
Then it rose past the wall line and was gone — leaving behind, in every mind simultaneously, the sensation that it hadn't entirely left. That it was observing from a position that simply wasn't visible.
Elv broke the silence first. "That was deeply unsettling."
"It was precise," CharliSpirit said, already thinking forward. "We need a decision framework. Not a leader in the traditional sense. A protocol."
"There's a meaningful difference?" Polaris asked, which was less a challenge than genuine curiosity.
"Yes," Revenant said. "A leader can be wrong and still decide. A well-designed protocol has structural resistance to bad decisions built in. The framework itself carries some of the weight."
"Nothing is fully wrong-resistant," Loyal2Schwab said without looking up from his junction mark. He had the tone of someone correcting for completeness rather than contradiction.
"Wrong-resistant," Revenant repeated. "Not wrong-proof. Those are different claims. I'm making the smaller one deliberately."
Galaxy had turned from the wall entirely and was watching the group now. "You're all arriving at the same answer from different directions," she said. "Someone should just say it."
"Say it then," Golden said.
"Someone needs synthesis authority," Galaxy said. "Clear input phase. Clear decision moment. Clear commitment after. The question is who synthesizes."
All eyes, in their own time, moved to Ninnginni.
Ninnginni had been building this in her head since before Soup arrived. When Golden spoke, it completed.
"Three stages," she said. "Input, synthesis, commitment. When we have a decision to make — any decision where we don't immediately agree — everyone speaks. Short, direct, one position each. No cross-talk during input. After input, I synthesize and make the call. After the call, we move. Disagreement before the call is not just allowed, it's required. Disagreement after the call costs us mobility, and mobility is what we need. If you think I'm wrong, you say so before. If I decide against your position and you're right, we review the outcome and adjust. The protocol learns."
"And if you make a call we think is genuinely dangerous?" Godly_Aura asked. He said it without aggression — it was the question that needed to be on the table.
"Then you tell me before I finalize. If you've made the case and I still decide against it, you comply and you document. When we have outcome data, we bring it back. I don't have ego about being wrong. I have investment in the process not collapsing when we're under pressure."
Revenant was quiet for a moment. "The framework requires trusting your synthesis."
"It requires trusting the process, which we all agree on," Ninnginni said. "That's a different trust. Smaller. More verifiable."
"The process requires your integrity," CharliSpirit said. It wasn't an accusation.
"Yes," Ninnginni said. "It does."
The silence that followed was not empty. It was ten people individually deciding whether they meant it — not performing agreement, but actually arriving at it, each in their own time and by their own reasoning. One by one, in different ways — a nod from lumallie, a spoken "agreed" from Polaris, a considered pause followed by a small exhale from Revenant that meant the same thing — they came to it. Loyal2Schwab didn't speak or gesture. He simply stood up from the junction mark and resumed walking forward. That was his version of yes.
Golden watched the group settle into agreement, eyes moving from one person to another.
"Good," they said finally. "Now we get to find out if we meant it."
Elv watched the last of this settle. "For the record," they said, "I'm noting that the moment we agreed on a framework, the tension in this corridor dropped by about thirty percent."
"Noted for the record," Ninnginni said, with something that wasn't quite a smile but was adjacent to one.
They moved. Faster now, and differently. The debate was gone, not suppressed — it had resolved into structure, and structure moved.
CharliSpirit led navigation with lumallie confirming at each junction. Polaris took point scouting — first through any unknown branch, reading the immediate path before the group committed. Revenant tracked elapsed time and called it at regular intervals. Loyal2Schwab maintained junction infrastructure, his marks now part of the team's shared map. Elv and Galaxy paired on wall pattern observation — looking for what wasn't consistent, for changes, for things the architecture wasn't advertising. Golden and Godly_Aura managed the rear, keeping eyes on what they'd passed through. Ninnginni moved at the center — receiving, processing, holding the whole picture.
"Input," she said, twenty minutes in, at a junction she didn't like. Three branches. CharliSpirit's map suggested straight ahead. Something about the air from the left branch felt different.
They gave it fast — each position in a sentence, clean. Straight ahead was mapped and confirmed. Left was unknown but something about the airflow suggested larger space. Right felt closed, short.
"Straight ahead," Ninnginni said. "We don't break confirmed data for atmospheric guess."
"Agreed," said CharliSpirit.
They moved.
Six minutes later, Galaxy stopped walking.
"This branch," she said, facing left at a junction. "CharliSpirit — was this here before?"
CharliSpirit came back. Looked at the map. Looked at the wall. Looked at the map again. Her expression tightened in a way that was specific to someone who was very confident in their work encountering a result that contradicted it. "No," she said quietly. "It wasn't here."
lumallie came up and confirmed the map, then confirmed the branch. "The map is accurate," she said. "The branch wasn't there when we passed."
"The labyrinth changed," Godly_Aura said.
"Or we mapped wrong," Polaris said. But his voice had lost the certainty it carried before.
"lumallie and I don't both misread the same wall," CharliSpirit said. No heat. Just fact.
Elv tilted their head slightly, studying the new branch.
"That's not random," they said. "It's timed."
Ninnginni looked at the new branch. At the known route. At the group's faces, which were running through the implications of an environment that could rearrange itself.
"Input."
It came fast. The new branch was unknown but potentially a shortcut or a test. The known route was confirmed correct but now longer given the new geometry. The wall change indicated active adaptation in the environment. Three people mentioned the possibility that the labyrinth was not static but responding.
Under ten seconds.
"Known route," Ninnginni said. "We don't chase new branches when the environment has just demonstrated it can move. We don't know if that branch is an opportunity or a manipulation."
They moved.
The structure at the labyrinth's center had been visible above the wall line for the last several minutes — its upper edge clear, its height larger than the initial estimate. Elv was watching it as they walked.
"It's bigger than it looked," Elv said.

Ninnginni didn't add to this. She was thinking about the wall that hadn't been there, and about what Soup had said about pressure, and about the difference between agreeing in calm corridors and agreeing in whatever waited at the center of whatever this was.
The protocol would hold. She'd made sure of that.
She hoped she'd made sure of that.

The ground had been fine when they woke up.
It continued being fine for approximately nineteen minutes.
Then a section of pathway eight paces behind Drug simply ceased. No sound. No warning. No gradual crumbling or audible crack. Clean edges appeared at the perimeter of a two-meter section of stone, and then the stone was gone, and below it was a depth that didn't show a bottom.

This was not a group that needed a long conversation to reach an obvious conclusion. The conclusion was immediate and unanimous: stillness was the threat. Forward was intact. Behind was disappearing. The viable survival model was forward, fast, and paying very close attention to everything.

NinjaFlout was counting. They had an instinct for pattern that expressed itself as measurement — pace counts, time intervals, repeated observations organized into something usable. "The collapses are averaging fifteen to twenty seconds apart," they reported without being asked. "But the intervals aren't consistent. Could be random. Could be something that only looks random."
"Random or patterned?" hollow Camillia asked. She was running beside NinjaFlout, watching the floor, watching the walls, dividing her attention evenly.
"Working on it," NinjaFlout said. "Keep talking to me. It helps me think."
"What do you want to know?"
"Everything. Nothing. Tell me what you're seeing."
"Canyon walls are the same height on both sides," hollow Camillia said, switching immediately to observation mode without breaking stride. "Joints in the stone run horizontal, not vertical. That's structural — they're less likely to crack sideways. The path itself is darker in the center than the edges."
"Darker in the center," NinjaFlout repeated. "Is that what janhk is seeing?"
"Maybe the opposite," janhk called back. "Warmer, I said. Which in this light might look lighter, actually."
"Hugging the edges then," NinjaFlout said. "Formation adjustment."
Rabbit moved the formation to the edges without calling it a command — just repositioning, and people followed, because the reasoning had been said aloud and the reasoning was good.
Mainthemainstar ran at the tail of the formation.
They hadn't said much yet. They ran with their head up and their eyes in wide-field rather than narrow-focus — not looking at anything specific, but maintaining full peripheral awareness like someone who had learned that the threat was usually in the thing you'd edited out. At one point, mid-canyon, Mainthemainstar said simply: "Right."
No explanation. No qualification.
The whole formation shifted right.
Twelve seconds later, the left edge of the path dropped away — cleanly, silently, exactly as the previous section had.
Nobody asked how Mainthemainstar had known.
They just ran.
The canyon opened. They spilled out onto a wide shelf of rock — open, elevated, with a stability that was immediately legible in the way the surface felt underfoot, a density that the canyon path hadn't had. The formation spread, instinctively, converting the tight run-shape into a broader survey pattern. Drug sat down immediately, with the decisive commitment of someone who had earned horizontal. janhk went straight to the perimeter, walking its edge methodically, testing with each step.
"Solid all the way around," janhk reported. "This one is real."
"Don't trust it fully," NinjaFlout said. Not paranoia — methodology. "But rest. We've earned two minutes."
"Five," Drug said.
"Five," NinjaFlout conceded.
Lyrix stretched both arms above her head and looked out at the terrain beyond the shelf — flat, open, wide, offering speed in every direction. Also offering no visual information about which sections would hold. "Is it worse when you can see the threat or when you can't?" she asked, not to anyone specific.
"When you can't," Kiwi said, settling beside her. "Visible threat is just a problem. Invisible threat is a problem and an anxiety. Two things."
"Deep thought," Drug said from the floor.
"I've had nineteen minutes of floor-disappearing and nothing else to process," Kiwi said. "Thoughts happen."
Akarimmu was standing at the far edge of the shelf, looking back at the canyon they'd come through. "We can't go back that way," he said. "Even if we wanted to."
"We don't want to," hollow Camillia said.
"Right. I'm establishing it as fact, not preference."
"Appreciated," Rabbit said.
The Killer Bunny appeared alongside JIKOSU at full sprint — though the sprint was now resting, so it appeared alongside him at stillness, which was somehow stranger. It materialized without announcement, white fur carrying a faint iridescent edge that shifted slightly as it moved, less like light reflecting and more like light being produced. Red eyes that focused on a point six seconds ahead of wherever you were standing rather than on you directly, which gave the impression of something that was always thinking slightly further forward than the present.
It settled at the shelf's edge and observed.
Nobody moved toward it immediately. The group watched it the way you watch something that has clearly arrived with purpose, and doesn't appear to be in any hurry to explain itself.
Rabbit walked to it.
"You see the collapses before they happen," Rabbit said. Not a question.
Not knowledge. Perception. The Bunny's communication arrived the way the animal itself moved — directly and without ceremony, sensation and compressed image more than language, but close enough to language to be usable. I pay very close attention to what is actually present, rather than what I expect to find. Most beings filter their perception through prediction. They see what they anticipated. They miss what is actually there.
"Can you teach us to do the same?"
I can show you what to attend to. Whether you can apply it depends on whether you're willing to stop trusting your assumptions more than your immediate sensory data. A pause, the Bunny's eyes shifting briefly to Mainthemainstar before returning forward. Some of you have already begun. The question is whether the group can develop it collectively, or whether speed becomes the universal answer to every problem you encounter.
For a fraction of a second, Rabbit hesitated.
Not physically — the movement didn't show — but internally, something caught.
Frucket's voice, distant but precise:
"If you move fast without seeing, you're just arriving at the wrong place quicker."
Rabbit blinked once.
Then the hesitation was gone.
"Speed is working," Rabbit said.
Speed is working on this terrain, against this particular threat, at this stage of the trial. Those conditions will not remain constant. The Bunny's left ear tracked something at the shelf's far edge — not threat, apparently, just information. You have time to develop something more durable than fast. Less time than would be comfortable. But some.
Rabbit turned back to the group.
"NinjaFlout — your interval count. Stay on it. Document the variance, not just the average." NinjaFlout nodded, already writing marks in the stone. "janhk — the texture observation. Teach it to Lyrix and hollow Camillia. Not the conclusion — the method. What you're actually looking at."
"I'll start now," janhk said, and moved to where Lyrix and hollow Camillia were standing, crouching at a section of the shelf's surface where the edge showed texture variation. "Look at this. Don't focus directly on the spot. Let your eyes go soft. The change reads differently in peripheral."
"I don't see it yet," hollow Camillia said, genuinely trying.
"That's right," janhk said. "The 'not seeing it yet' is the starting point. Keep going."
"Mainthemainstar," Rabbit said.
Mainthemainstar looked up from the edge of the shelf where they'd been sitting, watching the terrain beyond.
"Keep doing what you're doing. Whatever it is."
A pause. Then a small nod. That was enough for both of them.
Akarimmu had been watching the exchange. "What is Mainthemainstar doing?" he asked quietly, not critically — actually wanting to know.
"Something none of us can fully explain yet," Rabbit said. "Which is why we don't interrupt it."
The five-minute rest extended to twelve. Twelve was all they could justify without the threat of something else appearing — the terrain ahead was open and unread and probably temporary. The Killer Bunny sat at the shelf's edge throughout all of it, facing outward, ears in intermittent motion, reading a frequency that nobody else had access to. Not yet.
"I hate not knowing what's solid," said Akarimmu.
Kiwi settled beside Drug at some point in the middle of the rest, looking at the open terrain ahead. "What do you think it wants from us?" Kiwi asked.
"The system? Or the Bunny?"
"Either."
Drug was quiet for a moment. "The Bunny wants us to learn something before we need it. The system..." They trailed off. "The system might want the same thing. Or it might just want to see what we do when we don't know."
"That's not reassuring."
"No," Drug agreed. "It's accurate, though."
Lyrix was still working through the texture observation with janhk, her eyes moving from unfocused to focused and back. "I think I see it," she said, with the hesitation of someone who didn't want to claim something they weren't sure of. "That section, slightly to the left — it's different."
janhk looked. "Yes."
"I'm getting it," Lyrix said. "Barely — but it's there."
Lyrix blinked. "Oh." Something shifted in her posture — the small recalibration of someone who had just discovered a new sense they hadn't known they had access to. "Oh, that's different."
JIKOSU stood. He'd been watching the terrain ahead for the full twelve minutes, cataloguing. Wide, open, offering speed in every direction, completely unreadable at this distance. More surface area meant more variables. More variables meant the Bunny's perception would be carrying more load.
"When do we move?" JIKOSU asked.
The Killer Bunny's left ear angled sharply toward the far right. Not a motion the group could have easily predicted or assigned meaning to on their own. Rabbit could.
"Now," Rabbit said. "Right flank first. Loose spread."
They moved.
Faster than before, and smarter than before — the difference was legible. The formation ran with more horizontal separation, more coverage, each person assigned to a different visual sector rather than all of them watching the same central path. NinjaFlout called time intervals at regular marks. "Interval shortening," NinjaFlout called. "Stay sharp." janhk and Lyrix called texture from their positions. hollow Camillia was still learning, but she was close — calling one warning late but calling it, which was better than silence. Akarimmu covered the environmental edges, the periphery of what might be relevant. Kiwi kept the rear, watching for collapses behind them, building the map of what was gone.
Mainthemainstar called nothing.
They shifted once, twice — direction changes without preceding words, smooth and absolute. The group followed both times without needing to understand them. The ground behind both shifts proved them right within seconds.
By the time the light shifted into its first approximation of evening, the Syndicate had covered ground that felt impossible given the threat density they'd moved through. They had also watched three promising-looking routes dissolve into absence while they were still deciding whether to use them. They were faster, and they were starting to understand that speed was not the same thing as seeing.
The Killer Bunny observed all of this. Its expression communicated something that existed between good and not good enough yet.
Both of those things were accurate. It wasn't disappointed. Disappointment required a different expectation. The Bunny had simply noted the distance between current and necessary, and found it workable.
Workable was what Day 1 was supposed to produce.
